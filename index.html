I'm Beck, a first-year science student at The University of Hong Kong. I study leukemia, at the lab bench and in the data.

Each hexagon is one adult with acute myeloid leukemia.

276 patients from the Beat AML 2.0 study, with one RNA sample each.

100 of them carry a mutation in one gene, NPM1.

That's 36% of this group.

In their cancer cells, the same genes switch on.

HOXA9 and MEIS1 run about three times higher. Across the genome, 1,019 genes changed.

Those genes are what a new kind of drug targets.

Menin inhibitors, first approved for NPM1-mutated AML in 2025, are designed to switch this program off.

Then I checked it in 111 different patients.

In a separate study (TCGA), the same gene signature still told the two groups apart, with an AUC of 0.99 out of a perfect 1.

Inside the 100, there's a second split.

About a third (37) lean toward a primitive, stem-cell-like program. The other 63 look more mature. Two research teams described these subtypes in 2021 and 2023, and the same split showed up again in TCGA.

And the two groups react differently to a drug.

In lab tests on patients' own leukemia cells, the primitive group was far more sensitive to venetoclax, a widely used AML drug. It's an early result, but it hints that one mutation may need more than one treatment plan.

Featured project, 2026

NPM1-mutated AML transcriptomics

NPM1-mutated AML is often treated as one disease. My analysis adds evidence that it holds at least two subtypes, which may respond to drugs differently.

What the analysis added

  • The NPM1 gene signature held up in a second, independent cohort.
  • A locked 33-gene panel called NPM1 status correctly in 108 of 111 patients from that second cohort (AUC 0.996).
  • Cell maturity explains only a small part of that signature.
  • The primitive subtype's apparent FTO signal comes from a hidden transcript, IFEX9, as a 2023 study proposed.

Can sesamin slow leukemia cells?

Faculty of Medical Technology, Mahidol University, 2025. With Dalina Tanyong.

Leukemia treatment in Thailand is often costly and hard on patients. Sesamin, a natural compound from black sesame, was already known to push two leukemia cell lines toward cell death. I wanted to know how much it takes, and whether that dose is safe for the rest of the body.

I treated NB4 (acute promyelocytic leukemia) and MOLT-4 (T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia) cells for 24 and 48 hours and measured survival with MTT assays. Then I reviewed published safety data for normal cells along the digestive tract, from the mouth to the liver.
